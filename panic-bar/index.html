A panic bar problem usually shows up as a door that won't unlatch from the inside, a bar that won't return to its normal position, loose trim, or a cylinder that turns but doesn't retract the latch. Sometimes the push rail is bent or the end caps are missing. Other times the outside trim will be damaged from attempted entry, or the vertical rod on a double door will bind. We see older Von Duprin and Corbin Russwin units, exit devices with electrical components like Detex or Adams Rite, and simple mechanical bars on aluminum storefronts. Customers call because staff can't clear an exit during a turnover, an alarm won't reset, or a panic bar is catching on the frame and not latching properly.

On site we start with a hands-on inspection. We check the push rail, end cap, strikes, latchbolt, and any visible wiring on electrified devices. If the device allows, we remove the trim and operate the mechanism by hand to see where it's binding. We clean and lubricate moving parts, tighten loose fasteners, and realign the strike or latch plate. If a cylinder is faulty we remove and test it, and if a vertical rod needs adjustment we loosen the dogging and set the rod so it operates smoothly. For electrified devices we verify power at the control and test the release function with the wiring intact.

Whether a job is straightforward or tricky depends on the hardware, the door condition, and access for our van-based workshop. A modern surface-mounted exit device on a steel or aluminum door generally needs simple parts or adjustments. Concealed vertical rod systems on heavy wood doors or retrofit jobs where the stile was not cut square can take more time. Electrified devices add complexity if the building wiring is old or undocumented. If the door frame is warped or the threshold uneven, even a new device can bind. Accessible replacement parts like standard latches, strikes, or cylinders usually keep the job simpler; custom or obsolete parts make it more awkward.

When we finish, the customer has a tested exit device that operates smoothly from the inside and secures correctly from the outside. We show how the bar works, whether it dogged or releases on command, and we demonstrate the cylinder if one was replaced. Before leaving we cycle the device multiple times, check strike alignment at both latched and unlatched positions, and verify any electrical connections are secure and insulated. We leave the door closing and latching properly and provide a brief explanation of what was done and any recommendations for follow-up, keeping all work performed on site from our van-based workshop in Georgetown while covering every town in Kent and Sussex County.
