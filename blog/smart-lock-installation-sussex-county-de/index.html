Before you order a smart lock, go stand at your front door. That's where smart lock installation in Sussex County, DE usually goes right or wrong. Is the door drilled for the lock? Does it close without a shove? Will the lock get a Wi-Fi signal from where it hangs? The lock is rarely what fails.

What is the 4-Point Door Check?

The 4-Point Door Check is what we run through before a smart lock goes on any door. We made the name up, but the checks are real.

PointQuestionWhat you're looking for
1. FitIs the door drilled for this lock?Bore size, backset, door thickness
2. AlignmentDoes the bolt slide freely?A door that closes without lifting or pushing
3. ConnectionCan the lock reach your network?Wi-Fi or hub range at the door
4. BackupWhat opens it when the power is gone?A real key, a code, or a power-up option

Point 1: Does the door fit the lock?

Most entry doors in the US have a 2-1/8 inch hole through the face and a 1 inch hole in the edge. The backset, which is the distance from the edge of the door to the center of that big hole, is usually 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inches. Smart deadbolts from the big brands are made for that layout, and plenty of them come with an adjustable latch that covers both backsets.

Older doors are the problem. A hole that's worn out, oversized or drilled off-center won't hold a smart lock square. A patched edge won't either. Neither does a steel or fiberglass door with a thin skin around the bore. A locksmith can often sleeve or reinforce the hole, but you'd rather hear that before the lock is out of the box.

So measure:

  • the width of the hole in the face of the door
  • the backset, from the door edge to the center of the hole
  • the thickness of the door

Write the numbers down and hold them up against the lock's spec sheet. Not sure how to measure? A photo of your current deadbolt with a tape measure next to it does the job, and you can send it with a service request.

Point 2: Does the door close without a fight?

A smart lock throws the bolt with a small motor. A regular deadbolt lets you lift the door, lean on it and wiggle the key until it goes. A motor can't do any of that. It tries, jams, and chews through its batteries while it does.

Here's the test. Close the door and turn your current deadbolt with one finger. If it moves easily, the bolt lines up with the hole in the frame. If you have to lift the door or push it shut first, it's out of alignment.

Coastal Delaware makes this worse. Wood doors swell in the humidity, and salt eats thin strike plates. We cover finishes and materials in our post on door hardware that survives salt air. The fix is usually small, like a shimmed hinge, a strike plate moved a fraction of an inch or a re-cut mortise. Do it before the smart lock goes on.

Point 3: Will the lock stay connected?

Some smart locks only talk to your phone over Bluetooth. Others join your Wi-Fi so you can lock or unlock from anywhere, and some use a hub or a standard like Matter or Thread. Which one you need depends on what you want from it.

Say you want to let a cleaner in while you're at work. Then the lock has to be online. Stand at the front door and look at your phone's Wi-Fi bars. One bar means a Wi-Fi lock will keep dropping out. A mesh node, or a hub near the door, fixes most of that.

The router matters too, because the lock is one more device on your network. The Federal Trade Commission's guide to securing your home Wi-Fi network says to use WPA3 Personal or WPA2 Personal encryption, change the router's default admin name and password, keep its software up to date, and set up a guest network. Do those when you install the lock and you've got the basics covered.

Point 4: What happens when the batteries die?

They will. Every smart lock runs on batteries, and batteries run out. What matters is what the lock does next.

A decent one warns you early, through the app or with a chirp. A better one keeps a real key cylinder, so a dead lock is a nuisance and not a lockout. Some models have two contacts on the outside where you can touch a 9-volt battery to get one more code in.

Before you buy, ask yourself these:

  • Does it have a key override, and where will you keep the key?
  • How does it warn you when the batteries are low?
  • Can you power it from outside in an emergency?
  • Who else has a code, and how do you remove them?

People skip that last question. Codes pile up: the cleaner, the contractor, the neighbor who fed the cat in July. Pick one day each season to go through the list and delete what you can't explain. What does the lock's grade tell you?

A smart lock is a deadbolt with electronics on it, so the deadbolt still has to be well made. In the US that's rated under the ANSI/BHMA system, where Grade 1 is the highest level of performance. Locks for homes are normally Grade 2 or Grade 3.

Locksmith Ledger's summary of the ANSI/BHMA residential standards says the A156.2 cycle test needs 800,000 cycles for Grade 1, 400,000 for Grade 2 and 200,000 for Grade 3. The impact tests step up the same way.

GradeMinimum test cycles (A156.2)Where you usually see it
Grade 1800,000Commercial doors, heavy use
Grade 2400,000Many residential entry doors
Grade 3200,000Basic builder hardware

That article dates from 2010 and cites the 2003 edition of the standard, and standards get revised. Check the grade on the maker's current spec sheet. A front door opens a dozen times a day, so we lean toward a Grade 2 deadbolt or better. If the box doesn't show a grade, ask the seller.

Renting in Delaware?

You can put a smart lock on a rental, with conditions. Under 25 Del. C. § 5509, a tenant may add a lock if:

  • the tenant notifies the landlord in writing and supplies the landlord with a key to the lock
  • the new lock fits into the system already in place
  • the installation does not cause damage to the door

A smart lock with a key cylinder is the easy case, because you hand over a key. A code-only model is harder to square with the "supply a key" part. Read your lease, send the notice in writing, and keep a dated copy. If you run a short-term rental, our guide to smart locks in a holiday rental covers changing codes between guests.

Frequently asked questions

Can any smart lock fit my front door?
Most fit a standard door with a 2-1/8 inch hole and a 1 inch edge hole. Older, patched or thin-skinned doors may need the hole sleeved or reinforced first. Measure the hole, the backset and the door thickness, then compare them to the spec sheet.
Does a smart lock need Wi-Fi?
Not always. Bluetooth-only locks work from your phone when you're near the door. If you want to lock or unlock from anywhere, or share codes while you're away, you need a model with Wi-Fi or a hub.
What happens to a smart lock when the power goes out?
Nothing, as long as the batteries are good. The lock runs on its own batteries, so a house power cut doesn't touch it. A dead battery does. Pick a model with a key override or outside power contacts, and change the batteries when the low-battery warning shows.
Is a smart lock less secure than a regular deadbolt?
It depends on the deadbolt underneath and on how well you protect your network and codes. A Grade 2 or better deadbolt, a properly set up router and a tidy code list make a sound choice for most homes.
Can I install a smart lock myself?
Plenty of people do. Trouble starts when the door is out of alignment or the hole is off, because then the lock binds and the motor struggles. A locksmith can fix the door and set up the codes in one visit.
Can renters in Delaware install a smart lock?
Yes, once the landlord has written notice and a key. The lock has to fit the existing system, and the door can't be damaged. Check your lease too.
